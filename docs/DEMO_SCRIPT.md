# indite demo video — the script, for approval

**Audience:** leadership, advisors, investors. People who will sit and watch, and who decide things.
**Length:** about 9 minutes 30 seconds. Deliberately patient — every result stays on screen long enough to read.
**Voices:** the founder's own, for both the narration and the Hinglish spoken into the app.
**Rule:** nothing is claimed that the screen does not show.

---

## Why these sections and not others

Every section answers "so what?" for a user **and** for the business. If a use case is not in this table, it is not
in the video.

| What a person actually suffers | What it costs the business | What indite does | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| You think in Hinglish; the keyboard only knows English. Autocorrect fights every second word | Slower replies, and people switch to voice notes that nobody can search | Dictate Hinglish directly, into any app | 2 |
| Typing a long message takes minutes. Speaking it takes seconds | Hours a week per person, on messages alone | Hold a key and talk; hands-free for long thoughts | 2 |
| Product and company names always come out wrong | Every dictation needs fixing by hand, so people stop using dictation | Teach it once in the Dictionary | 3 |
| A client call ends and the notes exist only in someone's head | Decisions and commitments get lost; minutes take 30 minutes to write | Drop the recording in: transcript, who spoke, action items | 4 |
| Writing formal English from a rushed or angry thought is hard | Messages get delayed, or sent badly | Say it however it comes out, pick the style it goes out in | 5 |
| Work conversations cannot go to a third-party cloud | Vendor review, a data agreement, and a "no" from infosec | Nothing ever leaves the Mac. There is no account to create | 6 |
| Dictation tools charge per person, per month | A per-seat line item that grows with headcount | No subscription. AI runs on the person's own account, or not at all | 6 |
| Travel, airports, bad network | The tool fails exactly when it is needed | Works with the Wi-Fi off. Shown on camera | 6 |

**Deliberately not claimed:** team features, cloud sync, or that it is never wrong. Section 3 shows it being wrong
and shows the fix, because that is more convincing than pretending.

## The script

`N` = narration, the founder's voice. `S` = spoken into indite, the founder's voice, fed in place of the microphone.
Timings are targets. Every typed result holds on screen for at least 4 seconds before anything moves.

### 1. The problem (0:00 – 1:05)

> **N01.** Here is something every one of us does, every day, without noticing. We think in two languages at once.

> **N02.** But the keyboard only knows one. So we type Hinglish in Roman letters, and autocorrect fights us on every
> second word. Or we give up and send a voice note that nobody can search later.

> **N03.** Speaking is about three times faster than typing. The problem has never been the speed of our hands. It is
> that nothing listens to the way we actually talk.

> **N04.** This is indite. It runs on this Mac, with no internet, and no account needed for now, and it turns
> spoken Hinglish into text, directly into whatever app you are already in.

*On screen: empty TextEdit, full screen, menu bar in shot. Nothing else.*

### 2. The core: dictation (1:05 – 3:05)

> **N05.** Hold the right Option key, and talk.

> **S01.** Kal ki meeting chaar baje hai, please sab ko reminder bhej dena.

*Hold the typed line on screen for 5 seconds.*

> **N06.** That is it. No window to open, no button to press, no app to switch to. The words land where the cursor
> already was.

> **N07.** Look at what it did with "chaar baje". It wrote the digit four, because that is how you would have typed
> it. Spoken numbers, times, amounts and dates all come out the way a person writes them, not the way they are said.

> **N08.** For a longer thought, tap the right Command key three times. Now your hands are free, and it keeps
> listening until you tell it to stop.

> **S02.** Client ke saath call ke baad, deck mein last quarter ke numbers update karne hain, aur Rohit ko Friday tak
> final version bhejna hai. Aur haan, pricing wali slide dobara dekh lena, usme last revision miss ho gaya tha.

*Hold for 6 seconds.*

> **N09.** One tap ends it, and the whole thought is typed in one go. That is the difference between dictation you
> use for a sentence, and dictation you actually think out loud into.

> **N10.** Changed your mind halfway? Press Escape and nothing is written at all. And if you need the last thing you
> said again, somewhere else, Control Command V types it again.

> **N11.** One more thing it does quietly: if the cursor is in a password box, indite types nothing, saves nothing,
> and tells you why.

### 3. Getting it right, and what to do when it is wrong (3:05 – 4:25)

> **N12.** Now the honest part. It gets about one word in seven wrong today, on clean speech. Every dictation tool
> has a number like this; most of them do not show it to you.

> **N13.** The words it gets wrong are the ones that matter most to us: company names, product names, the people we
> work with. So, watch what happens when it gets one wrong.

> **S03.** CleverTap wala dashboard abhi tak load nahi ho raha hai.

*The mistake stays on screen. Then: Dictionary page, type the correction, add it.*

> **N14.** You teach it once, here, and it never gets that word wrong again. Not in this app, not in any app, not
> next week.

> **S04.** CleverTap wala dashboard ab theek chal raha hai.

> **N15.** The same list fixes your colleagues' names, your product names, and the short forms only your team uses.
> It takes ten seconds, once.

### 4. Beyond typing: calls, meetings and who said what (4:25 – 6:25)

> **N16.** Everything so far was about one person talking. Most of our work is not.

> **N17.** A client call ends. The recording is sitting in a folder, and the notes are in somebody's head. Drop the
> recording in here.

*File transcribes on screen; let it run visibly.*

> **N18.** It writes down the whole thing. And without being told how many people were in the room, it works out who
> said what, and separates them.

> **N19.** You can give the speakers their real names, export the whole thing as text or as subtitles, and search it
> later — which is the thing a voice note in your pocket can never do.

> **N20.** It also records meetings live: your microphone is you, the call's own sound is everybody else, and both
> are kept apart even when you are wearing headphones.

> **N21.** And anything in a note can become a to-do, in one click.

### 5. Your own AI, on your own account (6:25 – 8:25)

> **N22.** A transcript is raw material. What people actually want is the thing they were going to write anyway.

> **N23.** So every note can be handed to AI — and this is the part that matters — **your** AI. Your own Claude
> login, or your own key. indite has no AI of its own and no bill of its own.

> **N24.** Meeting notes: decisions, action items, open questions. Action items on their own, each with who owns it
> and by when. A summary as long as the note needs. Lecture notes with revision questions. Or a brain dump, organised.

*Show one running. Let the wait be visible; do not cut it.*

> **N25.** "Improve the wording" tidies it up — and your original stays right beside the new version. Nothing you
> actually said is ever thrown away.

> **N26.** "Rewrite to send" is the one people use most. Say it however it comes out of your head, then choose how it
> leaves: a formal English email with a subject line, a polite follow-up, a WhatsApp message in casual Hinglish, a
> Slack message, a LinkedIn post, an apology, a thank-you. Thirteen of them.

> **N27.** That is a real daily problem solved: the gap between how fast you can think in Hinglish and how long it
> takes you to write it in careful English.

> **N28.** Translate does the same across thirteen languages — Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali, Gujarati and more —
> so a note meant for a regional team does not need a second person to rewrite it.

> **N29.** And this one is for anyone who works with AI all day. Think out loud about what you want, and indite turns
> that rambling into a properly structured prompt — role, context, task, constraints, output format — ready to paste
> into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.


### 6. Why this one and not a cloud tool (8:25 – 9:15)

> **N31.** Now the reason this exists at all, instead of a subscription.

*Menu bar: Wi-Fi switched off, on camera. Then dictate again, uncut.*

> **S05.** Yeh poora demo abhi internet ke bina chal raha hai.

> **N32.** The Wi-Fi is off. The speech never left this Mac — not to a server, not to us, not to anyone. There is no
> account needed for now, no sign-in, and nothing to approve with infosec, because there is nothing to send.

> **N33.** There is no per-person subscription either. The dictation costs nothing to run. AI is off until you turn
> it on, and when you do, it runs on your own account.

> **N34.** Which also means it works on a plane, in a basement, and on the office network that blocks everything.

### 7. The ask (9:15 – 9:35)

> **N35.** This is working today, on this machine, built as a side project. What it needs next is not more features.
> It needs a handful of people willing to use it for a week and tell me where it breaks.

> **N36.** indite. Speak Hinglish, get text, and keep every word on your own Mac.

## What you record

| Set | Count | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **N01 – N36** | 36 lines | The narration, in your voice. Read normally, as if explaining to a colleague |
| **S01 – S05** | 5 lines | The Hinglish spoken *into* indite. Say these the way you actually talk — these prove the engine works on a real voice |

**⚠ Remember:** S01–S05 must be spoken naturally, not read carefully. The whole point is that it is real speech.

## Things to decide before recording

1. **The company name in section 3.** "CleverTap" is a placeholder — use a name your audience will recognise, and
   one the engine really does get wrong. We test it first and keep whatever it genuinely produces.
2. **Section 4 uses a sample two-person recording.** If you would rather use a real call, it has to be one everyone
   in it is happy to have on screen.
3. **Numbers quoted:** "one word in seven wrong" is the measured 14.4% on a clean test set. If you would rather not
   put a number on camera, say so and the line changes to "it still gets words wrong".
